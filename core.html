The DP Core

TOK · the Extended Essay · CAS

Three components every IB Diploma student completes — alongside their six subjects. The core is what makes IB more than the sum of its courses. It's where Hawks learn to think, write, and serve.

The core isn't a single class — it's three different commitments, woven across the two years of the Diploma Programme. Together, they shape a student who can ask hard questions, sustain long projects, and take action in their community.

Theory of Knowledge

TOK is the philosophical heart of the IB. It's a class that asks one big question over and over again: "How do we know what we know?"

Hawks examine knowledge in every form — scientific knowledge, mathematical knowledge, ethical claims, historical interpretation, religious belief, indigenous knowledge, the arts, technology, politics. The class isn't anti-science or anti-religion or anti-anything. It's pro-thinking.

What you'll actually do in TOK

  • Read short philosophical and journalistic pieces
  • Discuss real-world questions (Is the news lying to us? Can mathematics be wrong? How do we know what's ethical?)
  • Analyze how different "areas of knowledge" build claims differently
  • Look at the role of perspective: yours, your community's, history's
  • Write about all of it — analytically, not opinion-piece style

How TOK is assessed

The TOK Essay

A 1,600-word essay on one of six prescribed titles published by the IB each year. Submitted in 12th grade. Externally assessed.

The TOK Exhibition

Choose three objects from the real world and use them to explore a question about knowledge. Written commentary (~950 words). Internally assessed, externally moderated.

Why TOK matters in college and beyond.

TOK is the closest thing to a college humanities seminar that exists in U.S. high schools. Students who go through it routinely report that their college freshman classes felt familiar — because they'd already been doing the work.

What TOK is not

  • It's not a religion class.
  • It's not a political class.
  • It's not a debate class.
  • It's not a "tear apart your beliefs" class.

It's a class about knowing. About being honest about how we got to the things we hold true. That's a skill every Hawk uses for the rest of their life.

The Extended Essay

The Extended Essay is a 4,000-word independent research paper. Hawks choose a topic, formulate a research question, find their own sources, and write the paper over roughly 11 months, supervised by a teacher.

For many IB students, the EE is the first time they've ever produced a piece of writing this long, this rigorous, or this self-directed. It's also the closest experience to a college research paper that exists in high school.

Structure

  • Subject area: Anchored in one of the IB subjects (English, History, Biology, etc.). You don't have to be taking that subject at HL — just at SL or above.
  • Research question: Narrow, focused, debatable. "How effective was X for Y in Z context?" not "What is World War II about?"
  • Length: 4,000 words. Going significantly over is penalized.
  • Sources: Real research. Primary sources where possible. Properly cited.
  • Reflections: Three required reflections during the process (about 500 words total) documenting how your thinking changed.

The EE timeline (typical)

Spring of 11th grade

Choose a subject area and a supervisor

Your supervisor is a teacher in that subject. They check in with you, push back on your thinking, and help you stay on track.

Summer between 11 and 12

Draft your research question and start reading

Most students do their heaviest reading and research in the summer. Library card + a quiet corner = a productive August.

Fall of 12th grade

Outline and first draft

First draft due to your supervisor around late fall. Expect feedback — and expect to revise.

Winter of 12th grade

Revisions and the final draft

Polish, refine, double-check citations. Final EE submitted electronically through the IB.

Examples of EE topics from IB students

  • History: "To what extent did the Bracero Program shape mid-century labor patterns in the Inland Empire?"
  • Biology: "How does soil pH affect germination rates in Phaseolus vulgaris (common bean)?"
  • English: "How does Toni Morrison use water imagery to represent memory in Beloved?"
  • Mathematics: "Modeling the spread of misinformation on social media using SIR epidemic dynamics."
  • Visual Arts: "How do Chicano muralists use color theory to encode political resistance?"
  • Psychology: "What role does bilingualism play in working memory among adolescents?"
The EE pairs with TOK for bonus points.

The combined performance on TOK + EE earns up to 3 bonus points on the total IB score (out of 45). A strong EE alongside a strong TOK essay can be the difference between a good score and a great one.

CAS — Creativity, Activity, Service

CAS is the part of the IB that lives outside the classroom. Over 18 months, Hawks engage with three strands of activity:

Creativity

Anything that involves making, performing, or designing. Music, art, writing, dance, cooking, video, game design, fashion — anything where you're the maker.

Activity

Physical movement — purposeful and ongoing. Sports teams, dance, martial arts, hiking, weightlifting, recreational soccer. Not just "I walked once."

Service

Unpaid, voluntary action that benefits others. Tutoring, organizing donation drives, volunteering at a shelter, helping at the food bank, contributing to a community project.

The 7 Learning Outcomes

By the end of CAS, every Hawk should have evidence — through reflection — that they've engaged with all seven learning outcomes:

  1. Identify own strengths and develop areas for growth
  2. Demonstrate that challenges have been undertaken, developing new skills in the process
  3. Demonstrate how to initiate and plan a CAS experience
  4. Show commitment to and perseverance in CAS experiences
  5. Demonstrate the skills and recognize the benefits of working collaboratively
  6. Demonstrate engagement with issues of global significance
  7. Recognize and consider the ethics of choices and actions

The CAS Project

One CAS commitment must be a CAS Project — a sustained, collaborative endeavor that lasts at least one month and ideally combines two or more of the three strands.

Examples of CAS Projects:

  • Organizing a community art mural (Creativity + Service)
  • Coaching a youth basketball league (Activity + Service)
  • Running a school-wide voter registration drive (Service + Creativity, through outreach materials)
  • Producing a documentary about local water-rights issues (Creativity + Service)
  • Designing and running a Saturday tutoring program for younger siblings (Service + leadership)

How CAS is documented

CAS isn't a logbook of hours. The old "150 hours" rule was retired by the IB — what matters now is quality of engagement and reflection. Hawks keep a CAS portfolio with:

  • A brief description of each experience
  • Photos, artifacts, or evidence
  • Reflections — short, honest, written
  • Mapping to the 7 learning outcomes

The CAS Coordinator at AVHS meets with every CAS student periodically to check progress. CAS isn't graded 1–7 — it's completed or not completed. Completion is required for the diploma.

For Hawks who already work, caretake, or volunteer at home: this can count.

If you tutor younger siblings, translate for your family, help at your church, work at a family business, or take care of a grandparent — some of that already meets CAS expectations. Talk to the CAS Coordinator. Your real life is not invisible to this program.

Why the core matters

The six subjects build knowledge. The core builds the person. By graduation, an IB Diploma student has:

  • Been asked, over and over, how they know what they know (TOK)
  • Sustained an independent intellectual project for nearly a year (EE)
  • Made something, moved their body, and served their community — and reflected on all of it (CAS)

That's not just college prep. That's the foundation of a citizen.
